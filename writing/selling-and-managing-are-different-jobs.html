Selling and managing are different jobs

March 2026 · 2 min read

Your top seller just got promoted to manage a team, but six months later the team is underperforming and your best rep isn’t closing deals anymore. This happened often when I was leading teams and was almost never the new manager’s fault.

Selling and managing are completely different jobs. Sellers live on a quick feedback loop where almost everything they do ties directly back to their revenue number. When you close a deal everyone knows it, you feel that momentum immediately, and the cross functional relationships and brand building all drive right back to that revenue.

The problem is, managing is nothing like that. You’re building people and systems that win deals without you in the room. You’re figuring out which seller behaviors to scale, which to get rid of, and which to coach through. You’re learning to let go of controlling everything because your team is going to do things differently and often imperfectly, and that has to be okay or you you’ll fail.

You’re also protecting your team from a lot of noise and distraction. I’ve heard middle managers called “shit umbrellas” because so much swirl comes down from leadership and the manager’s job is to figure out what the team needs to hear and what they definitely don’t. You’re managing up to multiple types of leaders while setting the emotional tone for a group of people who are watching you, and if you’re stressed they feel it and it bleeds into everything.

I’m not an advocate of faking calm or being toxically positive because it’s not authentic and people see through it immediately, which ruins trust fast. What people need from their managers is not a performance of confidence, but someone who’s honest with a clear sense of direction. I was always real with my team even when things were hard, but I didn’t just dump it on them. I showed empathy and vulnerability, I related to what they were feeling and together we came up with plans. And oftentimes I said “I don’t know the answer to that, but I know how we can find out.”

One of the hardest parts about managing is that you almost never get the same recognition. When a big deal closes the seller gets the attention and the manager’s impact gets diluted or dismissed as “they just got lucky with a good team” or “the account list had tons of upside.” The positive feedback loop that kept you going as a seller basically disappears.

So next time you promote your best seller and give them none of this context, no training, no coach, no adjusted expectations, you’re not setting them up to succeed. You’re hoping they figure out a completely different skillset on their own. And that skillset is often very hard to teach because a lot of it is considered “soft.”

If you’re a seller thinking about moving into management, be honest with yourself about what that means. It’s not just a promotion. It’s a completely different job where your impact is harder to measure and slower to show up.

It’s also been the most rewarding part of my career to-date.
