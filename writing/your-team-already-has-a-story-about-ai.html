Your team already has a story about AI

June 2026 · 1 min read

Meta spent months tracking how its employees worked, down to every last keystroke, and recently it cut about 8,000 of those jobs to help pay for AI.

Almost everything I’ve seen since has been about the people who got pushed out, and a lot less about the ones still there, who had to watch it happen and then show up at work the next morning. Business as usual, I’m sure.

There was a piece in The Atlantic this month by Ellen Cushing called The Rise of Emotional Surveillance. It covers companies using AI to keep tabs on how their people feel, reading the mood in their Slack messages, scoring how into it they look on a video call, and in one case putting headsets on fast food workers to rate how friendly they sound.

The surveillance and the layoffs aren’t two separate stories. Companies watch how everybody works, all of it feeds the AI, and then the same people doing the watching get to decide who it can do without.

Your team is watching all of this too, trying to make sense of it. So the next time you bring in some AI tool and tell them it’s going to make their lives easier, their gut instinct won’t be to believe you. They already have a version of what AI is for, and you didn’t write it.

For a revenue leader, this is the real problem, because most of your job runs on trust (at least, if you do it well). You need it so people will tell you which deals are real and which aren’t, and you need the bad news as early as you can get it.

If you rolled out a new AI tool to your team next week, what would your team actually think you did it for?
